Synthetic Trust: AI Companions, Digital IDs, and the Backlash Ahead

Created on 2025-09-29 14:31

Published on 2025-09-29 15:58

This weekend, three seemingly unrelated stories lit up corners of the internet. And by lit up, I mean, captured the hearts and minds of our sub-four-second-attention-span populace for just enough time for sophisticated rage-baited ... discourse and debate.

I jest.

There were lots of comments, likes (dislikes), reshares -- and of course, botnet amplification.

So what were these stories? And what do they tell us about the struggle to define trust, companionship, and freedom in our hyper-connected AI Age of Acceleration?

1. “Friend” or Foe?

A startup called Friend.com launched a subway ad campaign for its AI-assisted wearable pendant.

First, what is ... "Friend?"

Put succinctly, Friend is an always-on companion that listens, learns, and provides company when no one else is around.

...Ah yes, because I want some-thing listening to my innermost thoughts, uploading them to a private company's servers, and analyzing my every move when no one is around.

So, this weekend, Friend.com launched a $1m NYC subway marketing campaign and the results were viral to say the least. I bet you can imagine the reaction!

The ads were met with immediate resistance. Never mind the fact that the company has yet to ship an actual product, many dismissed it as a surveillance tool wrapped in the language of companionship.

Why invite another device into your most private thoughts when trust in platforms and data custodianship is already at an all-time low?

2. Digital ID Backlash

In Switzerland and the UK, debates over digital ID systems are intensifying. Supporters argue they offer efficiency, fraud protection, and secure access to services. Critics see them as gateways to centralized surveillance and the erosion of civil liberties.

This weekend also saw a pivotal moment in the digital identity debates:

Switzerland narrowly approved its new e-ID Act: 50.4% yes, 49.6% no.

Unlike the 2021 version, this system will be state-run, optional, and free. Still, opponents called it a slippery slope toward centralized surveillance.

Meanwhile in the UK, PM Keir Starmer revived the idea of mandatory digital ID cards for workers. No “BritCard,” no job.

Over 1.6 million people signed a petition against it, warning it’s less about fraud prevention and more about mass surveillance. Like, the West's version of China's Social Credit Score or something...

So we have two models: Switzerland’s voluntary e-ID and Britain’s mandatory BritCard. Both are being pushed as “convenience.” Both are being resisted as some dystopian trojan horse for centralized control and surveillance.

The question is, how much convenience are we willing to trade for freedom?

And in a future that seems to necessitate some form of digital ID, how do we ensure these systems are implemented responsibly, with proper (decentralized) ownership and governance?

3. Vibes: Meta's "Slop Trough"

Meanwhile, while all the adults in the room were debating digital IDs, Meta quietly rolled out a new AI-generated content feed, which it calls "Vibes."

Think of it as TikTok for folks of retirement age, but if TikTok was powered solely by machines spitting out algorithmically produced noise.

Critics wasted no time: one described it as a “predator drone straight to the mind of Facebook users.” Another called it simply what it is — a slop trough.

The idea: endless filler content, optimized not to connect you with people, but to keep you scrolling. Because nothing says “community” like an infinite buffet of algorithmic sludge.

The bigger question: if our feeds become machine-made noise, what happens to human creativity?

Or to reality itself?

The Common Thread

On one side: AI as companion. On the other: AI as credential. And in the middle: AI as content machine.

All three provoke resistance because they touch the most sensitive parts of our humanity:

  • Friendship: Who do we trust with our inner life?

  • Freedom: Who do we trust to govern our identity?

  • Focus: Who do we trust with our attention?

The answer is NO ONE! Silly Rabbit.

The public’s reaction shows a growing perceptual instinct: to push back when intimacy, autonomy, and agency feel under threat.

My Take

AI adoption isn’t failing because it can’t simulate friendship, streamline transactions, or generate content. (Though, according to MIT, 95% of AI pilots do fail.) It’s failing because we don’t trust the stewards of these systems.

Until intimacy, identity, and attention are designed with human dignity at the core, the backlash will only grow louder.

That means reinventing three things:

  1. Governance: who sets the rules.

  2. Ownership: who controls the data and the models.

  3. Business model: how these systems are incentivized.

It is my personal belief that no single individual, corporation, or government should own the rights to your identity, ideas, or innermost thoughts.

Because in the end, the most radical technology we possess is still our own humanity.
